PixelDoll / environment reference / round two, October 22, 2026

One pixel-art aesthetic for characters and worlds

Explore fully pixel-art backgrounds that share the characters' visual language. The current generated scenery simulates a 3D space and reads as 2.5D beside the crisp pixel-art actors. Matching the world's pixel scale, edges, detail and overall aesthetic to the characters may make the whole scene feel more cohesive, welcoming and comfortable.

Current request: consider the world artwork only. Keep the current production UI and behavior. Grid lines, debug overlays and the historical UI shown in these screenshots are not part of the desired style.

Aesthetic references: Moonwell Grove and Ember Outpost show the consistency sought between characters and their environment. Their forest and courtyard settings are examples; no particular biome, named world or exact recreation is selected.

Possible routes: evaluate suitable itch.io assets, PixelLab generation or paying a pixel artist to create backgrounds. The desired result is consistent pixel art; a sourcing method has not been chosen.

This records a possible future background replacement, not an approved implementation. The old UI is documented below so its lessons and rejected choices remain clear.

The world artwork, without the UI

These original background plates illustrate a shared pixel-art aesthetic with the characters, rather than prescribe a forest or courtyard. The screenshot controls and grid overlays were added separately by the study and should be ignored when judging the scenery.

Moonwell Grove

A moonlit forest clearing in navy, teal and muted green. Trees, rocks, water and small plants frame a wide open center. Chunky shapes and darker distant trees give the actor a place to stand without filling the dressing area with scenery.

Lesson: the quieter of the two scenic concepts and the stronger historical recommendation for welcoming character selection and casual dressing. Keep perimeter detail out of unusual hats, capes and weapon silhouettes.

Ember Outpost

A dusk courtyard with a timber lodge, distant mountains, wooden fencing and restrained amber lantern/fire accents. Desaturated mauve ground leaves a broad empty stage while the perimeter suggests an inhabited world.

Lesson: stronger warmth and sense of place, with more visual competition from architecture, paving and warm light. A handcrafted interpretation should keep that atmosphere while simplifying material detail and protecting outfit color judgments.

Provenance: both plates were generated as original ImageGen scene studies on October 22, 2026. They are not existing handcrafted tilesets. Their value here is the visual direction; production art could be sourced, generated with suitable PixelLab tooling or commissioned separately. The source records call the original tool ImageGen; no specific internal GPT image-model identifier was exposed.

Original scene briefs and what they actually establish

The Grove prompt requested a moonlit clearing, stepped conifer silhouettes, pond, stone path, navy distance and an almost empty central 70%, using a nominal 16-color palette. Outpost requested a dusk courtyard, timber lodge, fence, mountains, tiny perimeter props and an empty lower/central 65%, using a nominal 20-color palette. Both requested chunky pixel art resembling a 320 × 180 scene enlarged to widescreen, with no characters or UI.

Those are prompt intentions, not verified native art specifications. The stored plates are both 1672 × 941 PNGs, and neither is a proven 16/32-pixel tile lattice or counted palette. No production-ready tile sheet was exported. The original art record preserves both complete prompts and output IDs.

The four other concepts were different: Calibration Yard, Blueprint Field, Claybox Courtyard and Test Islands use original code-authored integer-coordinate geometry in study.js. The study draws scenery and fixture sprites onto the same low-resolution canvas with smoothing disabled. It uses 4× desktop dressing, 3× phone dressing and 2× test-world or short-screen scale. That implementation is evidence of the old study, not a proposed replacement renderer.

Historical UI and workflow lessons

The research covered the whole creator, not only scenery. These lessons explain the old images and retain useful constraints; this task does not adopt their UI.

The broader 2D creator research used Stardew Valley for frame-dependent layering, Terraria for appearance versus functional equipment, MapleStory for whole-outfit previews, Starbound for body/slot/pose separation, Sun Haven for explicit None choices, RPG Maker for multi-view/layer output and Picrew for contextual part controls. These were comparative observations and design inferences, not evidence that PixelDoll should copy a game's inventories, commerce, unlocks or asset catalog.

Why the UI changed later

Round two's historical recommendation was Calibration Yard for everyday inspection, with Moonwell Grove as a welcoming collection backdrop. The subsequent review rejected the tool-like framing: a solid header, boxed name card, category rail on the opposite side from items, outlined stage, grid/debug controls, multiple bottom bars and a smaller actor overwhelmed the sense of a world.

The important distinction was composition and hierarchy, not a verdict against pixel art. The first-round Moonlit Camp reference supplied continuous scenery and a prominent character; round three restored that immersive hierarchy and later work consolidated it into Obsidian / Porcelain Bronze. This new reference revisits the scenic pixel-art language of Grove and Outpost while retaining the current UI.

  • Keep a continuous world behind the existing identity, equipment and utility controls. Keep dependable local backing for text where scenery is busy.
  • Preserve today's Preview / Top-down / Side-scroller structure, live movement, camera, touch ownership, focus and reduced-motion behavior.
  • Preserve current direction/clip fallbacks and matching equipment timing. Round two's four-view sample and mirrored west do not authorize new mirroring, invented directions or missing-frame synthesis.
  • Movement must not freeze just because an animation is missing. Live fallbacks are distinct from actual coverage reported by Preview, quality review and exports.
  • Current design excludes new actor lighting or shadows. Existing source shading stays untouched; reflection remains a code-only presentation choice.
  • Do not restore old wizard steps, movement buttons, Grid/Bounds/Proof controls, fixed test-room borders, props, combat or jumping as part of a scenery replacement.

The round-three critique explains the rejection. The accepted design record resolves later behavior corrections, and production experience describes the implemented integration. Historical documents do not override those records.

What a future background-only evaluation needs

Deferred board task: Consider pixel-art backgrounds that match the characters (Later).

The deferred idea is to possibly replace the current 2.5D-style generated scenery with fully pixel-art backgrounds that match the characters' style and aesthetics. The goal is a more consistent, welcoming and comfortable scene. Grove and Outpost demonstrate the difference in visual language; neither is a required environment. The current plates' recorded provenance is ImageGen. Grid lines are excluded from the desired style.

Possible ways to obtain the backgrounds

Route to evaluatePossible goalWhat needs checking before choosing
itch.io assetsFind existing pixel-art backgrounds or a small compatible tileset that fits the characters' aesthetic, with adaptation where needed.Pixel scale, palette, scenery density, required views, cost and whether the specific license permits the intended use and redistribution in this open-source app. No pack is selected.

Compare the routes by visual consistency, fit with the current renderer, quality, cost and usable rights. The proposal does not require handcrafting everything or commit to a provider.

  1. Define the visual match: compare environment pixel scale, edge treatment, detail, palette and sense of depth with real character artwork. Choose setting and subject matter later. A dark/light pair and mode-appropriate compositions still need decisions; Grove and Outpost were not designed as a matching dark/light theme pair.
  2. Choose a sourcing route: evaluate itch.io assets, PixelLab and an artist commission against that visual goal. Whatever the route, aim for clean pixel clusters, quiet ground and restrained detail. Tile-based construction is optional. Visible grids are not a goal.
  3. Check the current renderer: production currently uses Obsidian and Porcelain stage plates and samples their material for continuous live floors. New scenic art needs appropriate continuity, horizon and projection handling; do not assume a PNG swap will produce a convincing walkable forest or courtyard.
  4. Keep the current UI and behavior: evaluate the art beneath the existing Bronze UI, camera and controls. Any separate renderer work would need its own scoped plan; no UI redesign is implied.
  5. Validate real characters: test multiple palettes, silhouettes, sizes, facings and held weapons in still and moving views. Source sprite pixels and export output must stay unchanged.
  6. Validate every composition: desktop, portrait phone, short phone and landscape; light/dark UI readability; selection, creation, Preview and both live views; world edges, repeating seams, cropping, movement cues and reduced motion.
  7. Keep the proposal deferred: the reference does not authorize production replacement, paid generations, new dependencies, third-party asset acquisition or changes to the current UI.

Current implementation references: production world artwork and hashes, scene configuration and continuous ground renderer. Shared UI appearance remains owned by Bronze UI.
